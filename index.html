About

What transfers

Film set, cosmetics startup, university lab, semiconductor company and vintage products company . On paper those are different jobs. In practice I have been doing the same three things the whole time: the vocabulary changes, the software changes, the underlying work does not. Tools are the fastest thing to learn and the fastest thing to go out of date. These are what I actually carry between rooms.

  1. 01

    Sitting in someone else's seat

    Scene design taught me to inhabit a character's position before deciding how their world should look. That is the same move as a user interview, and the same move as reading a room of stakeholders who each hold one piece of the picture. You work out what someone needs before you work out what to build.

    Eleven film crews · patient interviews · stakeholders eight hours ahead
  2. 02

    Visualization & storytelling

    Whether it is a frame, a prototype, or an interface, the job is the same: put the thing where the eye goes first. You never get to stand next to someone and explain your intent — either it carries or it does not. Five years of scene design taught me to compose for the person in the seat, and the habit did not stay in the cinema. A cluttered screen and a badly framed shot fail in exactly the same way, which is when the person on the other end has to work too hard to find the point.

    Storyboards and scene composition · Figma prototypes · documentation teams actually adopted
  3. 03

    Turning messy input into a decision

    Qualitative research is not the soft option. You hear the same sentence from four different people and suddenly you are holding a decision rather than an opinion. The craft is in the compression: taking interviews, literature, and numbers that disagree with each other, and handing someone something they can actually act on.

    Thematic analysis · usability testing · KPIs reported whether or not they flattered us

Which is why I am not especially restricted to job titles. Give me a problem where somebody has to understand a person, decide something on evidence, and then make it clear to a room, and I have done that in four industries and three languages.

2025 — 26 ASML Change Management Coordination · Research

Twelve plates in the air, across two time zones

ASML runs on machines that take years to build and rooms full of people who each hold one piece of the picture. I sat in the middle of several projects at once, and the job, mostly, was making sure nobody lost the thread. Plans, milestones, and the open questions that everyone could see but nobody wanted to name out loud.

The one I am proudest of is Korea. I scoped it, ran it, and delivered it end to end on my own, with stakeholders eight hours ahead of me. That project taught me to flag a risk while it is still boring, and to rewrite a plan without treating the rewrite as a failure.

I also joined the UX Competence group and wrote the documentation and user guides that helped teams across the organization actually adopt the new way of working. It turns out a surprising amount of change management is just writing clearly.

A decision meeting goes well or badly depending on what you handed people three days earlier.

  • Ran one international project independently, scoping through delivery
  • Facilitated cross-departmental workshops and knowledge-sharing sessions
  • Turned stakeholder discussion into owned actions with named follow-through

Korea taught me more than any seminar did. Mostly that "send it and hope" is not a stakeholder strategy.

2025 — 26 Tilburg University MSc Communication & Information Science Research · Craft

A heart app, a chatbot, and a client who counted

My master's was the year I stopped guessing. I designed and prototyped a health app for cardiovascular patients in Figma and ran the full cycle, from user research through content design to usability testing, then ran it again, because the first version was wrong in ways only a real patient could show me.

Alongside it I studied how people actually talk to chatbots rather than how we assume they do, and turned those patterns into interaction guidelines a team could build from. I also ran a data-driven campaign for a government client with real KPIs attached, which is an excellent cure for falling in love with your own ideas.

  • Full case study: My HyprT medical app →
  • Full case study: chatbot dialogue design →
  • Campaign for a government client, with KPIs set and tracked

Round three of the prototype was the one where testers stopped apologizing for not understanding it. That is the real result.

2023 — 24 NES Cosmetics Marketing Strategist Research · Coordination

Startup speed, and the first time research changed a plan

Small team, fast decisions, everything happening at once. I took multi-platform campaigns from brief to delivery and kept several workstreams moving without letting any of them quietly stall, which in a startup is most of the job.

I also sat down with actual customers for long interviews. That was where I understood that qualitative research is not the soft option. You hear the same sentence from four different people and suddenly you are holding a decision, not an opinion. Then I tracked what happened after we shipped and reported it back, whether or not it flattered us.

  • Campaigns run brief to delivery under concurrent deadlines
  • In-depth consumer interviews turned into structured recommendations
  • Performance tracking and reporting for stakeholders

First time I brought a finding to a founder and watched the plan change because of it. Fairly hooked after that.

2018 — 23 National Chengchi University BA Communication, Taipei Craft

Eleven short films, and where to put the camera

I specialized in visual storytelling and scene design. Over five years I worked on more than eleven short films with international crews, which in practice meant long nights, borrowed equipment, and a great deal of arguing about where to put the camera.

What stayed with me is the discipline of sitting in the audience's seat. You never get to stand next to someone and explain your intent. Either the frame carries it or it does not. I have not yet found a product question where that stops being true.

You do not get to explain the frame to the person watching it.

  • 11+ short films with international, multilingual crews
  • Visual composition, scene design, and audience-first thinking

Four languages on one set, and still the same problem as any product team: everyone is certain they said the thing clearly.
